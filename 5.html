Leadership

Team Week
Team Week was my very first week at i.c.stars — and I was terrified. I felt like I wasn't smart enough to be there. For the first time in a long time I was completely out of my comfort zone: scared to talk, scared to make noise, fully in my shell.

One activity pushed me harder than anything else. We were paired with a stranger, blindfolded, and had to guide each other through an unknown space — up stairs, around corners, through obstacles — then switch and be guided ourselves. I already don't like being touched by people I don't know. And then, blindfolded and disoriented, we hit a section with fans. The noise, the inability to see, the unexpected touch — it was completely overwhelming.

I had to stop. I took a few seconds — maybe a few minutes — to breathe. But I kept going. I finished the course. And that moment taught me more about myself than I expected: that even when a fear I've carried for a long time shows up in full force, I can still push through it. That's when I knew this program was exactly where I needed to be.

Tea Host
Being a Tea Host taught me that it's okay to be nervous — because nervousness means you're growing. I learned how to ask questions in a way that opened people up rather than put them on the spot.

Instead of asking "what do you fear?" I learned to reframe it: Have you ever felt imposter syndrome? Simple questions like what's something interesting to you? or what book has changed your perspective? — those questions reach people in a way formal ones never do.

The biggest thing I took away: it does not matter what title someone holds. Every person in that room is a human being just trying to make a paycheck and live their life. You can talk to anyone — exactly the way you'd talk to a stranger you just met. That realization made me a better communicator, a better listener, and a less intimidated version of myself.

What I discovered is that my deepest goal isn't just a title or a salary. When I picture myself 25 years from now standing at that podium, I'm being recognized for one thing: getting people in the door.

I want to be what i.c.stars was for me — for someone else. Someone who wanted to enter technology but didn't have the resources, the connections, or anyone showing them the way. Someone who felt like they didn't belong. I know that feeling. I want to be the person who reaches back and says: you belong here, come with me.

My plan is to work my way up — starting from the helpdesk, earning my place, learning every layer. But the ladder was never just for me. If one door opens for me, I'm pulling someone in right behind me.
Portfolio · Section Two

Business

Client Project — KCPS
The Business Problem
KCPS had too many IT support tickets. Staff were flooding the helpdesk with issues that could be resolved independently — if they had the right resource. The goal was to reduce that workload by giving users a self-service solution.

Our Approach
We followed the Software Development Life Cycle — starting with user stories and UML diagrams, moving through wireframes and mockups, then building and testing the application in Power Apps and SharePoint. We presented our solution to KCPS stakeholders at every stage.

My Individual Role
I built the back-end of the application — the logic, data structure, and the SharePoint List that powered our Single Sign-On credential system. I was the Quality Engineer on my team, responsible for making sure everything worked and met the standard before it went in front of the client.
Consulting Company — CYBERSMITE
Our consulting company was CYBERSMITE — Top Tier Source for Tech Solutions. We built our identity from scratch: the name, the brand, the mission, and the vision.

Our mission was to empower others through practical services and personalized tech solutions designed to meet unique needs and drive long-term success. Building that from nothing taught me that a team's identity matters. When everyone believes in what they are building together, the work is better.
CYBERSMITE
Proposal Presentation & RFP
I built the website based on the constraints and goals outlined in the RFP. Every decision — the layout, the logic, the user flow — was tied directly back to what KCPS asked for and what their users needed.

I was nervous standing in front of those stakeholders. But I delivered. I knew the product because I built it. I walked them through it with confidence and answered what they asked. That moment — going from the person who came underprepared to the first Q&A to the person who stood in front of KCPS sponsors and held her own — that was growth you can measure.
KCPS Sponsors & Stakeholders
Working directly with KCPS sponsors taught me lessons no classroom can replicate. Two things stuck with me:

Always communicate with your stakeholders.
Do not wait until the end to find out you built the wrong thing. Check in. Ask questions. Keep them in the loop at every stage.

Know your client.
Before you walk into any room — know who they are, what they do, and what they care about. That research is not optional. It is the foundation of every professional relationship.
